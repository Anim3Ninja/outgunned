Evelyn Sullivan is 27 in 1934, a working-class Chicago woman who talked her way into the newspaper business and earned a place on its investigative beat. She’s blonde, lively and easy to talk to. She likes people, which is one reason she’s good at her work. Even when someone has very little to say for themselves, she can usually find something she’d like to ask them.

### The society pages

Evie knows why her editor first hired her. She was young, pretty and presentable enough to get into a charity ball or a formal dinner where some of his other reporters would have been stopped at the door. He wanted names, engagements and a few paragraphs about the dresses. She gave him what he asked for and paid attention to who spoke to whom after the introductions were over.

A photograph from a Chicago gala in 1931 shows her at 24, hair done up and wearing what was probably her only good evening dress. She has a practical leather bag slung over one shoulder, her notebook open and a newspaper photographer beside her. An announcer is calling out the guests’ names as they arrive. She looks quite at home in the room. What she’s really doing is putting names to faces.

The society beat taught her a good deal about influential people in Chicago. Some turned up in company she wouldn’t have expected. She began following those connections herself, working her way into corners of the city’s criminal world and coming back with stories her editor could put well ahead of the society column. She wants a byline people look for and would dearly love to win a Pulitzer.

### The story she published too soon

Early in her investigative career, Evie got a story linking a respectable Chicago businessman to organised crime. She was keen to see it in print. The reporting held up, and one detail she’d included let the people involved work out who had spoken to her. Someone had trusted her with information and had to live with being identified.

She takes more care now. She checks what can be corroborated and thinks about what a published detail might tell someone who already knows half the story. She’s willing to argue with an editor over a source’s name. A nervous witness usually finds her easy to talk to. She can put someone at ease, remember the two words they hesitated over and come back to them later.

Off duty, Evie likes dancing, champagne and the company of men she finds attractive. She’s comfortable with a romance lasting as long as the two people involved are enjoying it. She might marry if she meets someone she wants to marry. She has plenty she wants to do in the meantime. She can have a genuinely good evening at a party and still notice which guest slipped out before dinner.

### The courthouse, 17 February 1934

Evie was covering an organised-crime hearing when police brought the defendants out of a Chicago courthouse. She was at the front of the press crowd, close behind the police line, with an open notebook in one hand and her pencil extended as she pressed a gangster for an answer. Another photographer caught the moment he drew a pistol. A second later, he shot her in the abdomen.

She was taken to St. Alden’s Hospital for emergency surgery. She spent just over two weeks there, was discharged on 5 March and still carries the surgical scar. Her final bill came to $473. On 6 March the hospital marked it paid in full. A note on the account says that a bank draft settled the balance on behalf of a private party whose identity was withheld.

### The favour

A few months later, a London solicitor wrote to Evie on behalf of an unnamed client. The person who paid her hospital bill was asking a favour. An expedition was going to Egypt to look for the Stone of the Nile, and her presence was requested as a journalist. Her travel and accommodation would be covered.

Evie feels she owes the stranger something. She’d also very much like to know why anyone in London took an interest in a Chicago reporter’s hospital bill and what they expect her to find in Egypt. There’s a good story in the expedition itself. There may be another in the invitation. Her curiosity has already put her in the way of a bullet, and she knows perfectly well how that sounds. She goes anyway.
