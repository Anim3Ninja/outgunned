Major Charles Edmund Hargreaves is 60 in 1934. He’s a retired officer of the Royal Warwickshire Regiment, a veteran of two wars and a widower living near Leamington Spa. He introduces himself as Major Hargreaves. Eleven years of retirement have given him no reason to change the habit.

### From Warwickshire to the Western Front

Hargreaves was born in Warwickshire in 1874 and commissioned as a Second Lieutenant in 1895. He served with the regiment’s 2nd Battalion in South Africa during the Boer War. In 1914 he went to France as a Captain with the 1st Battalion. He was promoted to Major the following year and earned the Distinguished Service Order for his leadership on the Western Front.

He came to regard command as a practical responsibility. Someone had to make a decision while the information was still incomplete, tell the men what was expected of them and answer for what happened afterwards. Hargreaves could do that. He had the nerve to take charge when things went wrong and the experience to know that the first report a man brought him was rarely the whole story.

After the fighting ended, he remained in Britain in staff and training appointments. His officer’s identity booklet, issued on 12 May 1921, records his rank, regiment and decoration. A photograph from Salisbury Plain the following year shows him briefing two junior officers over a map spread across the bonnet of a staff car. He’s in his element there, working through the ground with men who’ll soon have to make decisions of their own. He retired in 1923.

### Eleven years out of uniform

Hargreaves lives comfortably on his pension and a modest private income. His home is full of campaign maps, framed photographs and regimental silver. He keeps his shoes polished and his moustache in order, dresses properly for dinner and takes particular pleasure in the company of people who remember the regiment as he does.

He can be pompous. He likes a good brandy and will readily tell a story that several people at the table have already heard. Time has improved some of the dialogue in his accounts of South Africa and made certain decisions sound rather more obvious than they were. He’s proud of his service and enjoys being asked about it. Retirement has made him sensitive to the possibility that the experience he spent a lifetime acquiring might be of less interest to the world than it once was.

A photograph from a private club dinner in Gibraltar in 1934 catches him in evening dress, brandy in hand, in the middle of a story. One of the younger officers is listening closely. Another guest has the look of a man who could supply the ending. Hargreaves is enjoying himself too much to hurry.

### The job in Egypt

Sir Reginald Harcourt asks Hargreaves to provide security for the expedition to find the Stone of the Nile. The Major is pleased to be asked. It’s been years since anyone outside his old circle wanted his judgement on a matter that might genuinely become dangerous.

He’ll begin by wanting to know who’s responsible for what, how far the party expects to travel and what arrangements have been made if somebody is injured. He’s accustomed to giving direct instructions and can sound condescending when a civilian questions him. He’ll sometimes press for a straightforward confrontation where somebody with more patience for intrigue might find another way through. That can make him difficult company when the danger is still a matter of conjecture.

When there’s a real emergency, the manner that irritates people over dinner can be reassuring. He gives people something clear to do and takes responsibility for the decision. Egypt gives him a chance to put that experience to work again, among people who’ll judge him by the decisions he makes in front of them.

“A little discipline, if you please.”
