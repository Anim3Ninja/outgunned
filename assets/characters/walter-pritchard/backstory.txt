Walter Pritchard is 26 in 1934, a Detroit-born air-racing champion, aircraft designer and one of the most recognisable faces in American aviation. He’s tall and athletic, with flowing beach-blond hair and a small, carefully kept moustache. He knows exactly how he looks stepping out of a cockpit. The people who’ve flown against him know how much work goes into making the whole thing look easy.

### The first aeroplane

Walter’s father made his fortune manufacturing automotive parts in Detroit. Walter grew up around workshops, engines and people who could tell a good piece of engineering from an expensive one. He was ten when the Great War ended. His enthusiasm for flying came from the air races, exhibition pilots and record attempts he grew up watching in the years that followed.

His father paid for the materials and workshop that got Walter started. Walter designed and built his first racing aircraft himself, entered it in a race and won. That first victory gave him prize money, a reputation and a reason to keep refining his designs. He has been making his own living from racing, sponsorships and exhibition flying ever since.

He gets on with his father when they see one another. There’s affection between them and a fair amount of distance. Walter knows what his father’s help made possible. He’s prickly about being introduced as the son of a wealthy industrialist now that he has trophies, a career and a name of his own.

### A pilot people come to watch

Walter moved to New York as his racing career took off. The airfields on Long Island gave him somewhere to fly and work on his machines. New York gave him sponsors, newspaper coverage and a social life that suited him very well. His civilian transport pilot’s licence dates to April 1932, with a renewal in February 1934. He has the judgement and confidence to get to grips with aircraft well outside his usual racing class, from single-seat machines to heavy transports and airships.

He understands what’s happening inside an engine as well as what it feels like through the controls. He’s always looking for places to shave a little more time off a run. That has won him races and brought him out of some extremely close calls. Afterwards, he tends to emerge from the cockpit grinning as though the whole thing had gone according to plan.

The signed photograph from Roosevelt Field in 1934 is Walter’s idea of a good afternoon preserved for posterity. His winning aircraft stands behind him, a silver cup sits on the grass at his feet, and there’s a promotional hostess on either arm. Across the print he’s written, Keep looking up!

### The company he keeps

In the spring of 1934, the society pages catch him arriving at a Manhattan aviation benefit in a white dinner jacket, a fashionable woman on his arm. Walter enjoys that sort of attention. He works at staying fit, dresses well and knows how to hold a room. It helps with sponsors, and he’d enjoy it just as much if there were no business in it.

Walter is an unapologetic womaniser. He likes the chase, likes making women laugh and will take a foolish risk if he thinks it’ll impress the one watching. He can be attentive and tremendous fun for an evening. Anyone hoping for a promise about the next one is likely to be disappointed. An attractive woman asking the right question can also get him talking more freely than he intended.

He treats social rivalry as a sport. Put him beside Edward Pembroke at a reception and Walter is quite capable of trying to outshine him before Edward has realised there’s a contest. Walter will enjoy himself either way. He may even congratulate Edward on a particularly good line before trying to top it.

### The race at Cairo

Walter is already in Egypt in 1934 to compete in an international air race around Cairo and the Pyramids of Giza. Sir Reginald Harcourt sees an opportunity and approaches him about the search for the Stone of the Nile. If the expedition finds it and needs to get away quickly, Harcourt wants a pilot who can fly them and the discovery out of Egypt.

Harcourt knows how to put the offer to him. There’s prize money to be earned back home any season. A flying job in Egypt that calls for someone willing to try what other pilots would think twice about comes along rather less often. Walter is accustomed to people hiring him for the name they can put on a poster. Here’s a man who wants him for what he can do in the air. That, and the chance to tell the story afterwards, is enough to get his attention.
