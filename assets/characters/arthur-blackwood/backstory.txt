Arthur Blackwood is about 43 in 1934. He spent roughly fifteen years investigating serious crime in London and rose to Detective Inspector at Scotland Yard. A gunshot wound to the shoulder brought that career to an end. He now works as a private enquiry agent, and anyone who knew him at the Yard would recognise the small leather notebook he still carries everywhere.

### His years at the Yard

Arthur earned his reputation through patient work. He could take a witness through the same few minutes of a story twice without making the interview feel like an interrogation. He knew when a discrepancy was worth pressing and when a frightened person simply needed time to get their thoughts in order. He had an eye for ordinary things that turned out to matter. A statement put a man on one street at a certain hour. A shopkeeper remembered seeing him somewhere else. Arthur would write both down and find out which account could stand up.

He’s quietly sceptical, particularly when somebody offers a very neat explanation before he’s had a chance to ask a question. His humour is dry enough to miss if you’re waiting for him to smile. He’s also learned how much an investigator can hear by letting a silence go on a little longer than feels comfortable.

### The injury and the private practice

Arthur was shot in the shoulder when an arrest went wrong. The wound healed, though the shoulder never regained its full movement. On an ordinary day the stiffness is easy to miss. He favours the other arm and has learned to spare himself the sort of exertion that will make the shoulder seize up later. He could still do the work he was best at. The physical demands of active police duty made staying on less and less practical, and he eventually left of his own accord.

His London practice is modest, with a battered filing cabinet and an ashtray that regularly needs emptying. Private work means fewer people to call on, and it suits him to decide for himself how to spend an afternoon. He still dresses respectably, favouring a well-worn dark suit and an overcoat that has seen plenty of London rain. He’s lean, with grey at his temples and the slightly tired look of a man who has spent too many evenings waiting for somebody to come home.

### Under observation in Holborn

One photograph in his dossier shows Arthur on a wet afternoon in Holborn in 1934. He’s standing under a tobacconist’s awning, watching a man come out of a boarding house across the street. The man carries a small leather case. Arthur has his notebook out and is making a brief entry as the man leaves.

The photograph was taken from an upstairs window. Arthur appears to be concentrating on the man across the road, unaware that somebody has found him worth watching too. The picture leaves both questions open. Who was Arthur following, and who wanted a record of him doing it?

### The enquiry in Egypt

Sir Reginald Harcourt offers Arthur a generous retainer to investigate the disappearance of the Stone of the Nile. An archaeologist can tell him where the stone belonged and what it meant. Arthur wants to know who saw it last, how they came to be there, and what they were doing in the hours they’ve left out of their account.

He accepts a paying case that gives him something worth finding out. In Cairo he’ll bring the habits that served him in London. He’ll talk to the person who opened the door as readily as the person who owns the house, and he’ll remember which of them answered a question he hadn’t asked. Harcourt may have hired him to look into a missing artefact. Where the enquiry goes from there will depend on what people tell him and what he can establish for himself.

“Now, tell me the part you left out.”
