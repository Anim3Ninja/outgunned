Josephine Mercer is 33 in 1934. She’s an African American freight driver and mechanic from Brooklyn who now makes her living hauling loads through Death Valley. Most people know her as Jo. Very few know where she picked up the name.

### Her father’s garage

Jo grew up with five brothers and a father who ran a garage. She learned mechanics at his side, working on the cars and engines that passed through the shop. She liked the work and was good at it. By the time her brothers went off to war, she already had a skill she could put to use. She wanted the chance to serve alongside them, even if getting that chance meant telling a convincing lie about who she was.

She enlisted under the name Joseph Mercer, passing as a young man and claiming to be older than she was. Her military identity card records her as Private Joseph Mercer, a motor transport driver aged 19. In 1918 she was actually 17. The men she served with called her Joe. When she came home, she kept the sound of the name.

### The runs she remembers

Jo worked in military motor pools, getting cars, motorcycles, lorries and even tanks back into service. At the roadside she learned to make do with whatever parts she could salvage. She was as useful behind the wheel as she was under the bonnet, and she took on runs carrying supplies and evacuating wounded men.

Some of those journeys stayed with her. Twenty or thirty men could be loaded into the back of a truck, and only a handful might still be alive when she reached the hospital. All Jo could do for most of them was keep driving. She remembers talking to the truck on runs like that, pleading with it to hold together for a few more miles. Years later, she still pats a dashboard and says the same thing, often with a grin.

Jo takes pride in what she did, and she’s kept it from her family. She can laugh along when veterans swap stories about foolish officers and broken-down vehicles. When the conversation turns to the wounded, she tends to go quiet. If she forgets herself and says something she could only know by having been there, she has a ready explanation. Her brothers told her. Two of them died in the war, and most people know better than to press her after that.

### Going west

Three of her brothers came home. The Depression hit the garage hard, and her father fell ill and died. One of her surviving brothers got the business going again. He knew Jo had worked alongside their father. He had no idea what she’d done in the army and saw little reason to listen when she told him how an engine ought to be fixed. To argue her case properly, she’d have had to explain where she gained that experience. She chose to leave Brooklyn with the secret intact.

By 1934 Jo has built a living of her own hauling freight through Death Valley. She takes loads along roads that can wear out a truck and a driver in the same afternoon. The occasional cargo is questionable. What keeps customers coming back is that Jo knows how to coax a heavily loaded vehicle through punishing heat and what to do when it finally gives out. She’s wiry and quick, accustomed to finding leverage or rigging a hoist when a job calls for more strength than she has.

A photograph from a roadside garage catches her just after she’s crawled out from under her truck, grease on her clothes and her flat cap turned backwards. A man has tried to tell her how to do the repair. She has a wrench in one hand and is giving him an answer he’ll probably remember. She’s had that conversation before, with people who knew her a great deal better than he did.

### The job in Egypt

Sir Reginald Harcourt needs someone who can move people and equipment through desert country and keep the expedition’s trucks running far from a garage. It’s paid work Jo knows she can do, with roads she’s curious to try. The people riding with her will soon learn what it means when she turns her cap around and grins.

She’s never quite shaken the feeling of having people in the back who are depending on her to get them somewhere alive. If somebody is hurt or stranded, Jo will push herself and the truck past the point where she’d normally think better of it. Afterwards she’ll find something on the engine that needs attention. It gives her a reason to keep her hands busy until she feels like talking again.
