Edward Pembroke is 33 in 1934. He is the younger son of a British viscount, an Oxford lecturer in Egyptology, and the young archaeologist credited with discovering a side chamber near Cairo in 1926. Eight years later, he still remembers the moment the chamber was opened more vividly than any lecture he has given since.

### The younger son

Edward grew up knowing what was expected of him. His elder brother would inherit the title. Edward would go to Oxford, make the right acquaintances and find a respectable place in politics, diplomacy or public life. The family had the connections to make any of those paths straightforward. Edward took to Oxford readily enough. What caught his attention there was a subject his family had never imagined him pursuing as a career.

He had been fascinated by ancient Egypt since childhood, when stories of pharaohs, tombs and lost civilisations first caught his imagination. At Oxford, he learned to read inscriptions, to make sense of the religious ideas behind them, and to pay attention to the details that could change the interpretation of a whole site. He found that the patient work of archaeology held his interest just as much as the stories that had drawn him to it.

His father could take some satisfaction in having a son who lectured at Oxford. An expensive expedition overseas was another matter. The Pembroke name gave Edward introductions, a comfortable upbringing and a standing in society that he could readily draw upon. His lecturer’s income gave him considerably less room to indulge his ambitions. A second excavation in Egypt would take a sponsor.

### The discovery outside Cairo

In 1926, Edward joined an excavation outside Cairo as a young scholar. His contribution was the discovery of a previously unopened side chamber. The surviving newspaper clipping shows him at the opening with a local labourer and a more senior archaeologist. The senior man naturally commanded much of the attention. Edward received credit for the find, and his name began to circulate among people who followed Egyptian archaeology.

The discovery was modest in scale and important to Edward in a way the newspaper could scarcely convey. Until then, Oxford and his family had set much of the course of his life. In Egypt he had followed his own curiosity and found something worth recording. He remembers the excitement of the work, the conversations afterwards, and how quickly a day could go by when everyone had another question they wanted answered.

Back in England, he lectured, researched Egyptian history and took part in British excavations when he could. He knows how a dig is organised and how easily a careless observation can spoil a useful piece of evidence. He can spend an afternoon making sense of a fragment of inscription and come away pleased with the day’s work. Even so, an English excavation has never quite satisfied his wish to return to Egypt.

### The gentleman at the table

Edward is charming, articulate and at ease in the rooms he was brought up to enter. His family still introduces him to suitable young women. He can give someone an entertaining evening, flirt quite happily, and leave her with the impression that he intends to call again, even when the question has scarcely occurred to him. The prospect of marriage holds some appeal if he meets someone whose company he genuinely wants. His family’s assessment of a suitable match carries very little weight with him on that point.

A good argument is one of the quickest ways to get his full attention. A person who can challenge his reading of an inscription interests him far more than someone who merely agrees. He could happily spend two hours discussing a burial custom with a labourer who had seen something he had only read about. Then an acquaintance might interrupt, and Edward would suddenly remember how he was expected to behave. The formality would return, sometimes awkwardly. His curiosity can carry him across social boundaries that his upbringing still teaches him to observe.

### What he takes to Egypt

Edward understands fieldwork as part of an organised expedition, with guides, supplies, tents and people who know the terrain. In a city, when trouble breaks out, his first instinct is to look for a policeman, a porter or whoever is responsible for keeping order. In the desert, it may take him a moment to realise that there is nobody to summon. A revolver thrust into his hands would leave him at a loss.

He has fenced and is more comfortable with a blade. His mother, worried about seeing him go abroad again, gives him a sword cane for protection. He values it because it comes from her. Drawing it in earnest would ask something of him that fencing never has.

Sir Reginald Harcourt’s invitation offers Edward the means to return to Egypt and a chance to contribute to the search for the Stone of the Nile. He has spent years wanting just such an opening. That eagerness makes it easy for him to take a well-connected patron at his word. Once he is in Cairo, he will have to decide for himself whose judgement to trust and what to do when there is nobody else to take charge.
