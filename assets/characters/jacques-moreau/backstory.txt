Jacques Moreau is 35 in 1934. He makes his living carrying freight around the Mediterranean in a small vessel that once belonged to his father. Marseille is his home port, and the police there have been keeping a file on him for years. If you ask Jacques what he does for a living, he’ll tell you he’s a merchant captain. He can usually say it with a straight face.

### A fisherman’s son

Jacques grew up in a fishing community on the northern French coast. His father made his living at sea until the German occupation of their port during the Great War brought tight restrictions on fishing and access to the harbour. The loss of that income put his family in difficulty. Still in his teens, Jacques found work as a deckhand aboard a French merchant vessel and sent what he could home to his parents.

During the war, a German submarine torpedoed his ship. Jacques escaped into the water and found a piece of wreckage to cling to. A separate German patrol vessel arrived after the sinking. As survivors struggled in the water and in the boats, its crew opened fire on them. Jacques spent the night clinging to timber before he was rescued.

He seldom tells anyone about that night. He can talk about the sinking if he has to. It’s what happened after the crew abandoned ship that he finds harder to speak about. He remembers men who couldn’t fight back and the sailors who shot them anyway. Years later, the sight of someone using a uniform or a position of authority to hurt a helpless person can make the usually affable Jacques frighteningly angry.

### His father’s boat

His father survived the war and eventually got hold of another small boat. Jacques worked with him for a time and later inherited her. By 1934 she’s an ageing coastal cargo vessel, small enough to call at harbours the bigger merchant ships pass by. Jacques knows her engine and all her peculiarities. He can be demanding with a crew aboard her, which sometimes surprises people who only know him from the waterfront bars. He has seen how quickly a mistake at sea can cost a life.

He discovered that undeclared tobacco, liquor and opium paid considerably better than ordinary freight. He takes legitimate cargo as well, and his work has taken him between French, Spanish, Italian and North African ports, with contacts as far east as Alexandria. He likes being his own captain. The boat gives him a living and lets him decide whose business he’ll take.

He’ll charge a wealthy customer an outrageous price if he thinks they’ll pay it. On a few occasions, he’s carried arms at cost for people fighting back against oppression. He makes that decision case by case. A cause has to earn his sympathy, and even then he’s unlikely to make a speech about it.

### The Marseille file

In 1933 someone photographed Jacques at a card table in a Marseille dockside tavern. A furious sailor was accusing him of cheating. Jacques had a pile of winnings in front of him, a cigarette and an amused expression. Below the table, where the sailor couldn’t see it, his hand was already resting on his revolver. The photograph catches how easily Jacques can keep up a friendly face while judging how badly a situation might turn.

The confidential police report, dated 18 December 1933, records years of suspicions about his cargo and associates without a conviction to show for them. The investigating officer, Henri Delacroix, added his own handwritten judgement.

> “Moreau is either an exceptionally fortunate merchant captain or an exceptionally accomplished liar. I favour the latter.”

By 1934 there’s also a photograph of Jacques on the Alexandria waterfront, talking quietly while men lower unmarked crates onto his boat by lantern light. It’s the sort of image that makes a police officer certain he’s right and leaves him struggling to prove what was in the crates.

### The job in Egypt

Someone connected with Sir Reginald Harcourt’s expedition obtained the Marseille report, had it translated into English and raised the question of whether Jacques could be trusted with an important job. His value to the expedition lies in the people he knows. In an unfamiliar port or city, he can find a reliable contact, arrange supplies or vehicles, and tell when a promising offer is likely to cost more than it seems. Harcourt is prepared to pay for that experience. Jacques is happy to be paid for it.

Under ordinary circumstances, he’ll laugh off an insult, flatter a man he despises and walk away with his fee. The expedition may find him harder to predict when someone has power over a person who can’t defend themselves. He has been known to spoil a perfectly good arrangement over that.

[“Mon ami, you wound me.”]
