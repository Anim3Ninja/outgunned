Sophie Whitfield is 29 in 1934. She’s an Oxford-educated researcher who specialises in ancient Egyptian texts and the restoration of damaged manuscripts. Among people who work in the archives, her name carries weight. Anyone who knows her chiefly through published papers could be forgiven for wondering how she’s spent the last few years.

### Oxford and the library

Sophie grew up in a comfortable English family. Her father, a successful accountant, could afford to give her a good education and help her through the gaps between research contracts. His support gave her room to build a career on her own terms. She knows how much difference that made, especially for women whose families expected them to give up paid work as soon as a suitable marriage came along.

At Oxford, she found that being admitted to the university gave her access to some parts of academic life and very little access to others. She took work in the library in her spare time. There she learned her way around the collections, became familiar with material she would seldom have encountered in lectures, and found that she enjoyed the painstaking business of tracking down evidence. After Oxford she was hired onto research projects, moving easily between libraries, archives and records offices. When a team needed someone to find an obscure reference or compare two apparently contradictory records, Sophie was the person they called.

### Learning to restore what she found

Conservation grew out of the research. A document she had spent weeks tracing might turn out to have faded almost beyond reading, or to be too fragile to handle in the ordinary way. Sophie taught herself how to examine damaged material, preserve what survived and recover enough of the text to make sense of it. In time, she became as valuable for what she could do with a manuscript on the table as for her ability to find it in the first place.

She has the patience to sit over a few lines of faded writing for an afternoon and the judgement to recognise when a missing fragment can only be guessed at. She arrives with her lenses, fine tools and notes properly packed. That habit carries over into the rest of her life. If Sophie has had time to prepare, she usually has whatever everyone else has just discovered they need.

### The British Museum, 1932

In 1932 Sophie restored and translated a damaged Egyptian papyrus that became the centrepiece of a small British Museum exhibition. At the private reception, the project’s senior scholar, Professor Alistair Reed, stood beside the display case and explained the importance of the manuscript. Most of the public credit had gone to him.

One of the guests asked about a damaged passage. Reed hesitated. Sophie stepped forward with her research folio, indicated the section in question and explained what could be read there. Several of the guests turned to listen. She kept her tone courteous and answered the question in full. Anyone who knew the work involved could see how thoroughly she knew the manuscript.

The episode was typical of the way her reputation spread. The people who had seen her work knew who had done it and recommended her privately. In print, the senior academic’s name was still the one people remembered.

### Independence and professional pride

Sophie supports the cause of women’s equality and regards equal voting rights as one gain in a struggle that has plenty of ground left to cover. She wants women to be able to keep their careers, receive credit for their work and make their own decisions about marriage. For herself, she has no desire to become a kept wife. She has put too much into earning her own living to agree to anything that would require giving it up.

Being talked down to can make her furious. Usually she keeps her temper long enough to ask the one precise question that exposes a mistake. She knows how easily a public quarrel with the wrong professor could cost her the next contract. That self-control serves her well, though pride can make her persist with a problem long after asking for help would have been sensible. Years of having her abilities questioned have made it particularly difficult for her to admit when she’s out of her depth.

### The invitation to Cairo

Edward Pembroke’s name crossed Sophie’s desk before Harcourt’s invitation arrived. She knows about the side chamber he discovered outside Cairo in 1926. From where she sits, the find seems fairly obvious, and the attention he received for it rankles. Her opinion of Edward rests on his reputation and on what she knows of the opportunities his class affords him. She’ll have the chance to judge the man for herself.

Someone familiar with Sophie’s work has recommended her to Sir Reginald Harcourt. She has limited expedition experience, but if the search for the Stone of the Nile turns up a brittle papyrus, an incomplete inscription or two records that tell different stories, she knows exactly where to begin. Cairo offers her paid work she wants to do and the chance to have her own name attached to what she finds.
