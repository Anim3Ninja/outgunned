Margaret Ashford is 35 in 1934. She served undercover in the Great War, stayed in British military intelligence after the armistice and rose to Staff Sergeant before leaving the service. She now takes on private work. To most people she’s a well-dressed Englishwoman with a good singing voice and a gift for making conversation. She can tell a great deal about a person from what they’re willing to say in front of her.

### Behind the lines

Margaret was recruited into British intelligence during the war. She worked with contacts in occupied northern France and Belgium and carried out assignments behind enemy lines. She speaks fluent German and learned to move through rooms where the wrong word would draw attention. She’s comfortable in muddy fatigues and can take the stage in an evening gown and hold a roomful of people through an entire song. Both abilities have kept her alive.

Her best deceptions often involve telling the truth. She really can sing, she really does know how to put a stranger at ease, and she can genuinely enjoy a conversation with someone whose business she’s there to find out. She lets people draw their own conclusions about her intentions. A man can leave an evening convinced he’s made an impression on her and remember only the next morning what he told her.

### Berlin, 1918

One of the oldest photographs in her dossier was taken in a Berlin cabaret in 1918. Margaret was about nineteen. Several Imperial German officers are visible in the room. She’s beside one of them in evening dress, looking towards the entertainment while slipping a folded document into her bodice. She lifted the paper from his uniform jacket. He’s still close enough to speak to her and has yet to notice it’s gone.

The photograph shows why her superiors trusted her with work that required composure. It also gives very little away about what the work cost her. She held dying comrades in her arms and sometimes had to get up and carry on with an assignment moments later. She became very good at getting through the immediate business. In the years since, she’s found plenty of reasons to keep putting off the rest.

### The Staff Sergeant

Margaret stayed in intelligence after the war, eventually taking charge of a small department. The service identity booklet issued to her on 14 March 1929 records her as a serving Staff Sergeant and department supervisor. Her hair is pinned up, her uniform is immaculate, and she’s looking directly into the camera. By then she had spent years in the field and was responsible for a department of her own.

By 1934 she’s left the service. She still has contacts from those years and takes discreet investigative and security work when it suits her. To a new client, she can present herself as a private investigator with useful connections. How she came by them is a longer conversation, and she rarely finds a reason to have it.

### Outside London

Margaret keeps a house outside London. In one photograph taken through the drawing-room window in 1934, she’s well dressed, alone in an armchair, with a generous glass of whisky and a half-empty bottle on the table. Leaves and branches obscure part of the view. Whoever took the photograph had climbed a tree in her garden. Margaret appears unaware of them.

At a reception she can spend an evening with a glass of champagne in hand, alert to every turn in the conversation. At home, whisky is what she reaches for when there’s nobody left to talk to and nothing urgent to do. She knows how to turn up looking composed the next day. She also knows how much effort that can take. She’s used to keeping other people at a comfortable distance, and there are few she’d willingly let see her on a bad evening.

### The job in Egypt

Sir Reginald Harcourt approaches Margaret about security for the expedition to find the Stone of the Nile. Her service record explains the invitation. Her experience makes her useful well before anyone has to defend the camp. At a Cairo reception or in a hotel bar, she can listen for the people asking unusually specific questions about the expedition and find out who’s been talking to them.

Margaret can be warm, funny and excellent company. She enjoys knowing more than she lets on, and there’s often a little mischief in the way she declines to answer a question. A travelling companion might spend weeks with her before working out which parts of her life she talks about freely and which ones she’s been steering the conversation around all along.
