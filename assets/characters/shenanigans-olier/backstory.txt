Shenanigans O’Lier is about 30 in 1934, an Irish-American expedition guide who works out of a small river-port town in the South American Amazon basin. She has red hair, freckles, battered boots and a habit of looking amused just when everyone else is beginning to worry. People hire her because she can get an expedition through country they’d never find their own way out of.

### Ships, caravans and paid trouble

Shenanigans grew up around ships and caravans. She learned early that a map could be wrong and a man who claimed to know the way could be worse. She makes a living paying attention to both. Traders, archaeologists and adventurers hire her when they need somebody who can read the country, follow a trail, find a missing member of the party and judge when it’s time to turn back. She’s equally comfortable talking to a porter, a dockside acquaintance or an official who wants to know what business she has being there.

She prefers to work on her own terms. She’ll listen to someone who knows their job and will expect the same courtesy when the ground turns difficult. A title or a uniform impresses her considerably less than sound advice. Her usual kit is a sawn-off shotgun, a machete she uses mainly for clearing paths, and a battered leather satchel. Given a minute to pack, she’ll bring the satchel and its useful assortment of maps, rope and odds and ends. If trouble comes without warning, she’ll get moving with whatever she has to hand.

### The Amazon rescue, 1932

In 1932, Shenanigans was guiding an expedition in the Amazon when rain upstream swelled a river the party was trying to cross. A rope had been stretched between the banks. Several people had made it across before Sebastian St. John-Frobisher, an explorer with a profitable sideline in writing about himself, lost his footing and let go.

Shenanigans went back into the water. An expedition photograph catches her holding on to the rope with one hand and hauling the soaked, panicked Frobisher with the other. People on both banks had stopped what they were doing to watch. For once, there was a camera present to record exactly who had needed rescuing and who had done the work.

Frobisher later published My Brush with Death upon the Raging Amazon! in his Sunday newspaper serial. Regular readers already knew him as the man who claimed to have seen the golden gates of El Dorado and gone beneath the surface of the Earth. In his telling, the river became more ferocious, the wildlife more monstrous and his own conduct considerably more dignified. He gave Shenanigans credit for pulling him out, while leaving readers with the impression that he had very nearly saved himself.

He also hinted that she visited his tent that evening for romantic reasons. She did visit his tent. She went to retrieve the bottle of whisky he’d helped himself to and was out again in under a minute. Frobisher got another conquest for his readers. Shenanigans got her whisky back.

### The people she gets attached to

A second photograph, taken in 1934 in the river port she works out of, shows her enjoying a quieter moment. Her boots are off, her hat and jacket are hung up, and her feet are on the veranda rail of a riverside hotel. She’s watching the waterfront with a drink in her hand. It’s the sort of afternoon she likes when nobody needs her to be anywhere.

She says she travels light and keeps her dealings businesslike. The collection of IOUs in her satchel gives a better account of her judgement where people are concerned. She has a particular weakness for rogues and lost causes. Someone with a convincing hard-luck story can talk her into extending a little more trust than they’ve earned. Once she’s taken responsibility for a person, she finds it difficult to leave them to their fate. Frobisher is hardly the sort of man she’d choose to spend an evening with. She still went into the river after him.

### The job in Egypt

Sir Reginald Harcourt is prepared to pay well for a guide who can take his expedition through difficult country in search of the Stone of the Nile. Shenanigans takes the offer and travels from South America to Cairo for the work, the money and the prospect of seeing what’s out there. As far as she’s concerned, the people paying for the expedition can decide what they’re looking for. Once they leave the road, she expects them to listen to the person who has to get them home.
